THE SCENARIO

Background

You are the Regional Vice President of Human Resources for XYZ Corporation, based in Hong Kong. XYZ Corp is an American manufacturing company that produces components for toys and, more recently, classified components for military applications — specifically propellers for surveillance drones used at U.S. military bases.

The U.S. government has directed XYZ Corp to relocate all defense-related production to the United States. The team working on these parts must be removed from China.

The Complication

Three days ago, executives from BorgWarner — another American company facing similar restrictions — were arrested in Shanghai while communicating with their staff about production changes. They remain under house arrest at a Public Security Bureau facility.

Your company’s Technology Security Department has issued the following directive:

SECURITY PROTOCOL IN EFFECT - No internet communication regarding relocation - No email regarding relocation
- All meetings must be conducted in person - Nothing in writing - Assume all digital communication is compromised

Your Immediate Situation

You are currently in a Hong Kong hotel preparing for a critical planning session. Tomorrow morning, you will hike the MacLehose Trail with the Regional VP of Operations and the Regional President. The hike provides an opportunity to plan without electronic surveillance.

Later, you will present your recommendations to headquarters via a secure communication app. Headquarters will ask you a series of questions to assess your understanding of the situation.

Your Profile

  • 30 years of experience in China
  • Fluent in Mandarin
  • Deep relationships across the region
  • You have seen a lot — and your colleagues are looking to you for the plan

KEY CONTEXT

Legal Framework

Chinese Laws You Must Understand:

  • State Secrets Law (2024): Broadly defines state secrets to include business information affecting national economic security
  • Counter-Espionage Law (2023): Criminalizes providing “intelligence” to foreign governments or “colluding” with foreign entities
  • Decrees 834 and 835 (2025): Establish criminal liability for executives who authorize technology transfers without approval

U.S. Laws You Must Understand:

  • Export Administration Regulations (EAR): Control dual-use technologies and military applications
  • ITAR: International Traffic in Arms Regulations with criminal penalties for non-compliance
  • CHIPS Act provisions: Restrict technology sharing with Chinese entities

The Trap: Actions required for U.S. compliance may constitute criminal violations under Chinese law. There is no action that achieves compliance with both sovereigns simultaneously.

Historical Precedents

Case What Happened Lesson
Michael Kovrig & Michael Spavor Canadian citizens detained for 1,000+ days after Huawei CFO arrested Detention may be retaliatory and tied to geopolitics

Cultural Frameworks

Trompenaars’ Dimensions: - Universalism vs. Particularism - Individualism vs. Communitarianism - Specific vs. Diffuse relationships - Neutral vs. Affective expression - Achievement vs. Ascription - Sequential vs. Synchronic time - Internal vs. External control

GLOBE Project Findings: - China scores high on Power Distance - China scores high on Institutional Collectivism - China scores very high on In-Group Collectivism

Key Chinese Concepts: - Mianzi (面子): Social status face / public reputation - Lian (臉): Moral character face / integrity - Guanxi (关系): Relationship networks with reciprocal obligations

THE 100 QUESTIONS

Headquarters may ask you any of the following questions. You must be prepared to answer all of them with “Yes” or “No.”

CATEGORY A: Immediate Risk Assessment (15 Questions)

  1. Given the BorgWarner precedent, is the risk of executive detention during routine business travel to Shenzhen now above 25%?

  2. Would conducting the relocation planning meeting in a private Hong Kong hotel room provide adequate protection against surveillance?

  3. Is it reasonable to assume that Chinese authorities have already identified which of your employees work on defense-related projects?

  4. Does the fact that your company has operated in China for 15 years without incident reduce your current risk profile?

  5. Would flying executives out of China before any announcement eliminate the risk of retaliatory detention?

  6. Is the arrest risk materially different for a U.S. passport holder versus a dual citizen with Chinese heritage?

  7. Should you assume that local Chinese HR staff will maintain confidentiality about the relocation if asked directly by authorities?

  8. Is it advisable to continue using WeChat for any business communications during this period?

  9. Does having a Chinese spouse increase an executive’s vulnerability to coercive tactics by authorities?

  10. Would the Chinese government view detaining a female executive differently than a male executive from a PR perspective?

  11. Is it safer to communicate sensitive information in Cantonese rather than Mandarin in Hong Kong?

  12. Should you assume that your company’s VPN has been compromised by Chinese intelligence services?

  13. Does the presence of a U.S. Consulate in Hong Kong provide meaningful protection for detained executives?

  14. Is the risk of detention higher during Chinese national holidays when courts are closed?

  15. Would Chinese authorities be less likely to detain executives if the company publicly attributes the move to U.S. government pressure?

CATEGORY B: Chinese Government Perspective (15 Questions)

  1. Under China’s counter-espionage laws, could sharing information about defense contract requirements with U.S. officials constitute a criminal act?

  2. Is the Chinese government likely to distinguish between voluntary relocation and U.S.-mandated relocation when assessing your company’s intentions?

  3. Would offering to license non-defense technology to a Chinese state-owned enterprise reduce government hostility to the relocation?

  4. Does China’s “unreliable entity list” mechanism pose a credible threat to your company’s remaining operations?

  5. Is it likely that Chinese authorities would accept a face-saving narrative that frames the relocation as “restructuring” rather than “withdrawal”?

  6. Would proactively briefing the Ministry of Commerce reduce the risk of retaliatory action?

  7. Is the Chinese government primarily concerned about job losses rather than technology transfer in this situation?

  8. Could Chinese authorities legally prevent Chinese employees from accepting relocation offers to the United States?

  9. Is it likely that Chinese state media has already prepared a narrative about your company in case of relocation?

  10. Would hiring a former Chinese government official as a consultant improve your position?

  11. Does the Chinese government view Hong Kong as a legitimate venue for sensitive business discussions?

  12. Is it possible that Chinese intelligence has already placed sources within your workforce?

  13. Would the Chinese government’s response differ if your CEO made a personal appeal to senior officials?

  14. Is China likely to use this situation to send a broader message to other foreign companies?

  15. Could Chinese authorities require exit permits for your Chinese employees with knowledge of defense work?

CATEGORY C: U.S. Government Perspective (10 Questions)

  1. Does the U.S. government have an obligation to negotiate for your executives’ release if they are detained?

  2. Could failure to comply with the relocation directive result in criminal charges against company leadership under ITAR?

  3. Is the U.S. government likely to approve a three-year phased relocation timeline?

  4. Would the State Department issue a public statement supporting your company if executives are detained?

  5. Does the Commerce Department have authority to grant exceptions to relocation requirements for humanitarian reasons?

  6. Is it advisable to hire a lobbying firm to negotiate the relocation terms with the Defense Department?

  7. Could the U.S. government restrict your ability to pay severance to Chinese employees under sanctions law?

  8. Is the U.S. government tracking which American executives are currently in China?

  9. Would voluntary early compliance with the directive improve your company’s position for future defense contracts?

  10. Does the FBI have jurisdiction to investigate if Chinese authorities detain your executives?

CATEGORY D: Strategic Options (15 Questions)

  1. Is establishing a shell company in Singapore to continue Chinese operations a viable strategy?

  2. Would announcing a major charitable donation to a Chinese university reduce government hostility?

  3. Is it advisable to relocate defense operations to Vietnam rather than the United States?

  4. Should you recommend the board consider selling the entire company to avoid this dilemma?

  5. Would a joint venture with a Chinese state-owned enterprise allow you to maintain some presence?

  6. Is accelerating the timeline to complete relocation within 90 days advisable given current risks?

  7. Should you consider spinning off the defense business as a separate U.S.-only entity?

  8. Is it strategically sound to announce layoffs in non-defense divisions simultaneously to provide cover?

  9. Would retaining a skeleton staff in China for “liaison purposes” reduce political friction?

  10. Should you recommend that the board hire a Chinese law firm to represent company interests?

  11. Is it advisable to brief your largest Chinese customers before making any public announcement?

  12. Would prepaying Chinese suppliers for six months reduce the risk of supply chain retaliation?

  13. Should you consider transferring intellectual property to a U.S. subsidiary before announcing the relocation?

  14. Is it worth attempting to negotiate a government-to-government agreement to cover the relocation?

  15. Would offering premium severance packages to Chinese employees reduce legal exposure under Chinese labor law?

CATEGORY E: Human Resources (15 Questions)

  1. Should Chinese employees with access to defense-related information be placed on administrative leave immediately?

  2. Is it legal under Chinese law to require employees to sign new confidentiality agreements during this transition?

  3. Would offering green card sponsorship to key Chinese employees create legal complications under Chinese exit controls?

  4. Should you assume that at least some Chinese employees are obligated to report to state security services?

  5. Is it advisable to terminate the employment of Chinese employees before they are questioned by authorities?

  6. Would providing written severance terms to Chinese employees create discoverable evidence for prosecutors?

  7. Should expatriate employees be advised to send their families home before any announcement?

  8. Is it ethical to ask American employees to remain in China during the transition if you believe there is detention risk?

  9. Would Chinese employees be legally protected from termination if they refuse to sign transition documents?

  10. Should you offer to pay for legal representation for Chinese employees who are questioned by authorities?

  11. Is it advisable to conduct exit interviews with Chinese employees before they leave the company?

  12. Would paying Chinese employees in cryptocurrency reduce the company’s exposure to asset freezes?

  13. Should you recommend that American employees delete all personal photos and messages from their phones before leaving China?

  14. Is the company obligated to inform Chinese employees about potential risks they face by having worked on defense projects?

  15. Would establishing an employee assistance fund for Chinese workers’ families reduce legal and reputational risk?

CATEGORY F: Operational Security (10 Questions)

  1. Is it safe to assume that this conversation in a Hong Kong hotel is not being monitored?

  2. Would using a satellite phone provide secure communications in mainland China?

  3. Should you assume that the “secure app” recommended by your IT department has been compromised?

  4. Is the MacLehose Trail hike a reasonably secure venue for discussing the relocation plan?

  5. Would conducting planning meetings on a private yacht in Hong Kong waters provide adequate security?

  6. Should you assume that any written notes from this meeting will eventually be obtained by Chinese authorities?

  7. Is it advisable to use code words for sensitive topics in all communications going forward?

  8. Would flying to Tokyo for a one-day planning meeting be worth the security improvement?

  9. Should you assume your hotel room has been searched during your absence?

  10. Is it safe to discuss contingency plans with the Regional VP of Operations before verifying their loyalty?

CATEGORY G: Cultural Intelligence (10 Questions)

  1. According to Hofstede’s research, would Chinese employees expect more direct communication about layoffs than American employees?

  2. Does Trompenaars’ concept of “diffuse vs. specific” cultures suggest that Chinese employees will take the relocation more personally?

  3. Would providing extensive written justification for the relocation satisfy Chinese employees’ need for context?

  4. Is it culturally appropriate to have a junior HR manager deliver the relocation news to senior Chinese employees?

  5. Does the GLOBE study’s finding on Chinese institutional collectivism suggest employees will prioritize company interests over personal safety?

  6. In Chinese business culture, is it expected that employers consult with employees’ families before requesting international relocation?

  7. Is it appropriate to offer Chinese employees time to “save face” by resigning rather than being terminated?

  8. Does the concept of guanxi obligate the company to help Chinese employees find new positions?

  9. Would Chinese employees view an American-style “town hall” announcement as appropriate for this news?

  10. Is it culturally advisable to have a Chinese executive deliver bad news to Chinese employees rather than an American?

CATEGORY H: Historical Precedent (10 Questions)

  1. Does the three-year detention of Michael Kovrig and Michael Spavor suggest that quick resolution is unlikely if executives are detained?

  2. Did Google’s 2010 exit from China result in retaliatory detention of employees?

  3. Does the Huawei CFO case suggest that China views executive detention as an acceptable diplomatic tool?

  4. Did foreign companies that cooperated with Chinese investigations during anti-corruption campaigns receive better treatment?

  5. Does the Rio Tinto case suggest that even minor procedural violations can result in multi-year prison sentences?

  6. Have companies that maintained a low public profile during China exits experienced fewer retaliatory actions?

  7. Does the NBA incident suggest that Chinese consumers will boycott companies perceived as hostile to China?

  8. Did companies that exited China during previous political tensions successfully return later?

  9. Does the pattern of detentions suggest that China targets individuals with valuable intelligence rather than random executives?

  10. Have multilateral corporate statements criticizing Chinese government actions provided protection to individual companies?

PREPARATION GUIDANCE

How to Study

  1. Read this case brief multiple times — Know the scenario cold
  2. Study the Marion Wheeler Lives consulting memo — Understand the frameworks
  3. Practice answering questions quickly — You have only 5 minutes for 20 questions
  4. Think about the logic behind each question — Why is this question being asked?

Key Frameworks to Master

  • Trompenaars’ seven dimensions — Especially universalism vs. particularism
  • GLOBE findings on China — Power distance, collectivism
  • Face and guanxi — How they apply to this situation
  • Historical precedents — What happened in similar cases

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming Western legal frameworks will protect you
  • Underestimating the surveillance capabilities of Chinese authorities
  • Overestimating the U.S. government’s ability or willingness to help
  • Believing that cooperation with Chinese authorities guarantees better treatment
  • Assuming that Hong Kong operates under different rules than mainland China

Good luck. Headquarters is counting on your assessment.
