PART I: THE LEGAL FRAMEWORK

1.1 Chinese Counter-Espionage Laws

State Secrets Law (Revised 2024)

The People’s Republic of China defines “state secrets” broadly to include: - Information concerning national economic and social development - Information concerning science and technology - Any other matters classified as state secrets by the state secrecy administration

Critical implication: Business information about supply chain relocations, production changes, or personnel movements may be classified as state secrets if deemed relevant to national economic security.

Counter-Espionage Law (Revised 2023)

This law criminalizes: - Providing “intelligence” to foreign governments or organizations - “Colluding” with foreign entities in ways that harm Chinese national security - Refusing to cooperate with state security investigations

Critical implication: Communicating with U.S. government agencies about compliance with U.S. export controls may constitute “espionage” under Chinese law.

Decrees 834 and 835 (2025)

These administrative decrees, issued by the Ministry of State Security, establish: - Mandatory reporting requirements for foreign companies regarding technology transfers - Expanded definition of “critical data” subject to export restrictions - Criminal liability for executives who authorize data or technology transfers without approval - Extraterritorial application to foreign subsidiaries

Critical implication: Personnel with knowledge of production processes for defense-related components may be subject to exit restrictions. Authorizing relocation without Chinese government approval may expose executives to detention.

1.2 U.S. Export Control Laws

Export Administration Regulations (EAR)

The Bureau of Industry and Security maintains the Entity List and controls exports of: - Dual-use technologies - Items with military applications - Technology transfers to designated countries or entities

CHIPS Act and Related Restrictions (2022-2026)

Recent legislation and executive orders restrict: - Semiconductor manufacturing in China by U.S. companies - Technology sharing with Chinese entities - Employment of U.S. persons in certain Chinese technology enterprises

National Defense Authorization Act Provisions

NDAA provisions increasingly require: - Domestic production of defense-related components - Certification of supply chain security - Reporting of foreign government interactions

Critical implication: Failure to comply with U.S. government directives regarding defense production may result in loss of government contracts, criminal prosecution under ITAR, and exclusion from the U.S. defense industrial base.

1.3 The Trap

The strategic trap emerges from the intersection of these legal frameworks:

Action U.S. Law Consequence Chinese Law Consequence
Relocate production per U.S. directive Compliance Potential criminal violation (technology transfer without approval, state secrets)
Refuse to relocate production Criminal violation, loss of contracts Compliance
Communicate with U.S. government Required for compliance Potential espionage charge
Communicate relocation plan to Chinese staff Required for employment law Potential data export violation
Silent exit of personnel May be possible Exit bans may prevent departure

There is no action that achieves compliance with both sovereigns simultaneously.

PART II: HISTORICAL PRECEDENTS

2.1 Detention of Foreign Executives in China

Pattern Analysis (2015-2026)

Our firm has analyzed 47 cases of foreign business executives detained in China. Key findings:

Trigger Events: - Attempted departure during sensitive business negotiations: 34% - Communication with foreign government agencies: 23% - Business disputes with Chinese partners: 21% - Technology or data transfer allegations: 15% - Unknown/political: 7%

Resolution Timelines: - Released within 30 days: 12% - Released within 6 months: 38% - Released within 2 years: 71% - Detained longer than 2 years: 29%

Resolution Mechanisms: - Diplomatic negotiation: 42% - Payment/settlement with Chinese party: 31% - Criminal conviction followed by deportation: 18% - Ongoing/unresolved: 9%

2.2 Notable Cases

Michael Kovrig and Michael Spavor (2018-2021)

Two Canadian citizens detained days after Canada arrested Huawei CFO Meng Wanzhou on U.S. extradition request. Held for over 1,000 days. Released within hours of Meng’s release.

Lesson: Detention of foreign nationals may be explicitly retaliatory and tied to geopolitical events beyond the individual’s control. Resolution depends on state-to-state negotiation, not legal process.

Stern Hu, Rio Tinto (2009-2014)

Australian executive detained during iron ore price negotiations with Chinese steel companies. Charged with bribery and stealing commercial secrets. Sentenced to 10 years, served approximately 6.

Lesson: Business negotiations that disadvantage Chinese state interests may be reframed as criminal conduct. Commercial disputes can become criminal cases.

GSK China Executives (2014)

GlaxoSmithKline executives detained on bribery charges during broader anti-corruption campaign. Mark Reilly (British) deported after conviction.

Lesson: Foreign executives may be detained during politically-driven campaigns where their company serves as an example. Individual conduct may be secondary to political utility.

Qualcomm Investigation (2014-2015)

While no executives were detained, Qualcomm was subjected to extensive investigation and ultimately paid $975 million fine. The investigation coincided with U.S.-China technology tensions.

Lesson: Companies in strategic technology sectors face heightened regulatory scrutiny that may not be proportionate to actual violations.

BorgWarner (2026 - Current)

U.S. auto parts executives detained in Shanghai while communicating with staff about production changes. Currently under house arrest at Public Security Bureau facility.

Lesson: This is your current operating environment. The threshold for detention has lowered. Communication of routine business decisions can trigger enforcement action.

2.3 Exit Ban Patterns

Distinct from detention, exit bans prevent individuals from leaving China while remaining nominally free. Our analysis of 23 documented cases shows:

Common Triggers: - Ongoing business disputes - Tax investigations - Pending regulatory approvals - “Assisting investigation” requirements

Duration: - Median: 8 months - Range: 2 weeks to 4+ years (ongoing)

Warning Signs: - Passport confiscation during routine inspection - Requests for “voluntary” meetings with authorities - Questions at border that result in “system problems” - Partner/counterparty filing of complaints

PART III: CULTURAL FRAMEWORKS

3.1 Fons Trompenaars: Seven Dimensions of Culture

Universalism vs. Particularism

United States: Strongly universalist. Rules apply equally to all. Law is paramount.

China: Strongly particularist. Relationships and context determine appropriate behavior. Loyalty to in-group may override abstract rules.

Application: American executives often assume that following proper legal procedures provides protection. Chinese authorities may view procedural compliance as irrelevant if the substantive outcome threatens Chinese interests or relationships.

Individualism vs. Communitarianism

United States: Highly individualistic. Individual rights, achievements, and accountability.

China: Highly communitarian. Group identity, collective responsibility, organizational loyalty.

Application: Chinese employees will expect the organization to protect them. Individual exits may be viewed as betrayal. Group communication is essential but also creates witnesses.

Specific vs. Diffuse

United States: Specific. Clear separation between work and personal life. Professional relationships are bounded.

China: Diffuse. Work relationships extend into personal domains. Business relationships involve whole-person engagement.

Application: Your 30-year relationships in China are diffuse relationships. They may provide protection—or create obligations. Using these relationships involves the whole relationship, not just the business dimension.

Neutral vs. Affective

United States: More neutral. Emotional restraint in business settings. Data-driven decision making.

China: More affective in in-group settings. Emotional expression signals trust and relationship depth.

Application: In crisis communication, emotional authenticity matters. Chinese employees will read your emotional signals. Overly corporate, neutral communication may signal distance or abandonment.

Achievement vs. Ascription

United States: Achievement-oriented. Status based on accomplishment.

China: Mix of achievement and ascription. Status based on position, age, relationships, and background as well as achievement.

Application: Your status as someone with 30 years in China carries ascribed weight. The Regional President’s title matters. Use status appropriately in interactions with officials and employees.

Sequential vs. Synchronic Time

United States: Sequential. Linear planning. One thing at a time. Deadlines are commitments.

China: More synchronic. Multiple processes run simultaneously. Time is flexible. Relationships matter more than schedules.

Application: Your timeline is not China’s timeline. Attempting to force rapid sequential execution may signal panic or disrespect. Build in flexibility while maintaining urgency.

Internal vs. External Control

United States: Internal locus of control. We shape our environment. Problems have solutions. Action orientation.

China: More external orientation. Harmony with environment. Adaptation to circumstances. Patience.

Application: Chinese counterparts may view aggressive action as foolish resistance to inevitable forces. Demonstrating awareness of constraints may build credibility.

3.2 GLOBE Project Dimensions

The Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness (GLOBE) project provides additional comparative data:

Power Distance

China: High (5.04 practices score) United States: Moderate (4.88 practices score)

Application: Chinese employees expect clear direction from senior leadership. Uncertainty or conflicting signals from leadership creates anxiety. Your communication must convey confident authority.

Uncertainty Avoidance

China: Lower (4.94 practices score) United States: Higher (4.15 practices score)

Note: This dimension measures societal practices, not individual preferences. Chinese organizations operate with higher uncertainty tolerance as a system, though individuals may feel significant anxiety.

Application: Chinese organizations may appear more comfortable with ambiguity, but individuals within them may be highly anxious. Don’t mistake organizational tolerance for individual comfort.

Institutional Collectivism

China: High (4.77 practices score) United States: Lower (4.20 practices score)

Application: Chinese employees identify strongly with the organization. Organizational crisis is personal crisis. Loyalty cuts both ways—they expect loyalty from you.

In-Group Collectivism

China: Very high (5.80 practices score) United States: Lower (4.25 practices score)

Application: Family and close group bonds are paramount. Decisions affecting employees affect families. Chinese employees will consider family implications before organizational implications.

Future Orientation

China: Moderate (4.73 practices score) United States: Higher (4.15 practices score)

Application: Long-term thinking matters in Chinese strategic culture. Short-term crisis response should be framed within longer-term relationship and strategy context.

3.3 The Concept of “Face” (面子)

Face operates on multiple levels:

Mianzi (面子): Social status face. Public reputation. External perception.

Lian (臉): Moral character face. Internal integrity. Trust-worthiness.

Application to Your Situation:

  1. Your company’s departure from China affects the mianzi of Chinese officials who approved your operations
  2. How you depart affects their lian—whether they are seen as having been fooled or treated with respect
  3. Your Chinese employees’ mianzi is affected by association with a departing company
  4. Providing face-saving options for all parties creates space for resolution

Face-Giving Strategies: - Attribute decisions to external forces (U.S. government requirements) rather than choice - Emphasize continued respect for China and Chinese colleagues - Create narratives that protect employee dignity - Consider whether formal/informal communication channels affect face

3.4 Guanxi (关系)

Guanxi refers to the network of relationships that enable business and social operations in China.

Key Principles: - Relationships carry reciprocal obligations - Trust builds through repeated exchanges over time - Guanxi can be transferred but not instantly created - Guanxi with officials creates both protection and exposure

Application to Your Situation:

Your 30 years of guanxi in China is both your greatest asset and your greatest exposure.

Asset: You have relationships that can provide intelligence, create communication channels, and potentially buffer negative actions.

Exposure: Your relationships are known. Using them for this situation may compromise relationship partners. They may be unwilling to assist due to their own risk.

Strategic Questions: - Which relationships can you activate without exposing the relationship partner? - What obligations do you have to relationship partners? - How do you protect those who have helped you over the years?

PART V: STRATEGIC OPTIONS FRAMEWORK

5.1 The Four Quadrants

Slow Execution Fast Execution
High Transparency Negotiated Exit Shock Transition
Low Transparency Quiet Restructuring Covert Extraction

Negotiated Exit (Slow/Transparent) - Engage Chinese authorities directly - Seek approval for restructuring - Extended timeline (12-24 months) - Risk: May result in blocked exit, detained negotiators

Shock Transition (Fast/Transparent) - Announce relocation publicly - Move immediately - Accept stranded assets and personnel - Risk: Immediate enforcement, retaliation

Quiet Restructuring (Slow/Low Transparency) - Gradual personnel rotation - Production wind-down masked as business adjustment - No formal announcement until complete - Risk: Detection during process, extended exposure period

Covert Extraction (Fast/Low Transparency) - Key personnel exit before announcement - Minimal advance communication - Announcement from outside China - Risk: Personnel who cannot exit, potential criminal charges in absentia

5.2 Decision Criteria

No option is clearly superior. Selection depends on:

  1. Risk tolerance: How much downside can you accept?
  2. Timeline pressure: How urgent is U.S. government directive?
  3. Personnel profile: How many high-risk individuals?
  4. Asset importance: What are you willing to lose?
  5. Relationship value: What is the long-term value of China presence?
  6. Legal exposure: What actions create personal criminal liability?

5.3 Recommendation Framework

For companies with: - Small defense-related operations - Most personnel low-risk profile - Significant ongoing China business - Long-term relationship value

Consider: Quiet Restructuring with contingency extraction plan for high-risk personnel.

For companies with: - Significant defense exposure - Multiple high-risk personnel - Limited ongoing China business - Urgent U.S. government timeline

Consider: Covert Extraction followed by external announcement.

PART VI: OPERATIONAL SECURITY NOTES

6.1 Communication Protocols

Assume compromise of: - All electronic communications (email, messaging, phone) - Hotel rooms and meeting spaces in PRC and Hong Kong - Personal devices - Cloud storage

Reduced-risk communication: - In-person meetings in verified locations - Written notes destroyed after reading - Third-country meetings where feasible - Coded language for electronic communication (limited utility if pattern recognized)

6.2 Personnel Protection

Before any announcement: - Verify exit capability of key personnel (border testing) - Position high-risk individuals in low-risk locations - Brief personnel on detention protocols - Establish emergency contact protocols - Consider family positioning

Detention Protocols: - Establish duress codes - Identify in-country legal resources - Establish diplomatic notification chain - Prepare holding statements - Brief families on communication protocols

The current operating environment in China represents a new paradigm for multinational corporations. The tools of the Cold War—containment, coercion, information control—are now applied in commercial contexts. Companies must operate as if they are in hostile intelligence environments while maintaining business operations.

There is no risk-free path. There is only risk management.
